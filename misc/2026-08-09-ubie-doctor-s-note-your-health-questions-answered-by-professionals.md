How is an absence seizure different from other types of seizures?

Absence seizures are a type of generalized seizure that cause brief, sudden lapses in awareness, often appearing as staring spells or "blanking out." Unlike convulsive seizures, they do not involve shaking, falling, or dramatic loss of consciousness. Episodes typically last only a few seconds, but they can happen many times a day and are most common in children. Because absence seizures can be mistaken for daydreaming or inattention, they often go undiagnosed for months or years — delaying treatment that could prevent learning difficulties, accidents, and progression to other seizure types. How is narcolepsy related to REM sleep?

Narcolepsy is a chronic neurological disorder directly tied to REM (rapid eye movement) sleep. Normally, REM sleep occurs about 60–90 minutes after falling asleep, but people with narcolepsy enter REM within minutes—both at night and during unintended daytime sleep episodes. This disrupted sleep architecture causes hallmark symptoms, including excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy (sudden muscle weakness triggered by emotion), sleep paralysis, and vivid hallucinations at sleep onset. How is REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD) different from sleepwalking?

REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD) and sleepwalking are two distinct parasomnias that occur during different stages of sleep. RBD happens during REM sleep, when a person physically acts out vivid dreams—often through kicking, punching, or shouting—because the normal muscle paralysis of REM sleep is absent. Sleepwalking, by contrast, occurs during non-REM (deep) sleep and involves walking or performing complex behaviors while unaware, with no memory of the event afterward. If you or a loved one are experiencing unusual nighttime behaviors, understanding the cause is the first step toward better sleep and safety. Both RBD and sleepwalking can signal underlying health conditions that benefit from early evaluation.
