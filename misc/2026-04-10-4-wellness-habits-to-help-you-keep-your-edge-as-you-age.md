# 4 Wellness Habits To Help You Keep Your Edge As You Age

ENTERING MIDLIFE CAN shift the way you think about your body. “Hitting 50 or 60 might feel like a turning point, but you’ve still got it,” says Leticia Acevedo, EdD, RN, a director of a nursing assistant program in North Carolina. “If you’re focused on healthy habits, it can make a considerable difference in how you age.”

Acevedo suggests starting with your immune system. As you get older, your immune system weakens, making it harder to fight infections, viruses, and even heal from everyday wounds. That’s why you don’t bounce back from illness as quickly as you did in your 20s. By taking the right measures, though, you can help shore up your defenses. Here are Acevedo’s top strategies for feeling young and strong at every stage of life.

1. Take steps to help keep preventable illness at bay.

HELP SUPPORT YOUR IMMUNE system by staying up to date on your recommended vaccines, says Acevedo. For example, flu, pneumococcal pneumonia, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and COVID-19 vaccines can help prepare your immune system to fight these illnesses.

“Some people might say they don’t need a vaccine because they never get the flu, or because they had COVID-19 and it was mild, but we don’t have the same strains of those infections every year,” Acevedo says. “These vaccines help you build up your defenses against new strains.”

For vaccines that are not given yearly—like shingles, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), or pneumococcal pneumonia—they work by helping to prepare the immune system to be ready to react when needed. “We can’t predict what our immune system will encounter, but we can help protect ourselves from certain illnesses that may come with complications as we age,” says Acevedo.

Talk to your healthcare provider or pharmacist about which respiratory vaccines are right for you. Plus, for those who want to save time, ask if you’re eligible for more than one vaccine when you’re getting your flu shot.

2. Dial in your sleep.

SLEEP IS CRUCIAL for a variety of functions from better cardiovascular health to improved brain processing, and immune function. When you’re asleep, the body produces immune cells that are essential for fighting infections and other threats. Sleep also helps regulate the body’s level of inflammation—another immunity bonus.

There are several ways to help improve sleep hygiene, including having the same bed and wake time each day (even on weekends), avoiding caffeine and big meals late in the evening, and developing a wind-down routine. But what often gets overlooked is how managing stress can set the stage for better sleep, says Acevedo.

“Any activity you can do during the day that makes you feel calmer and more balanced is helpful to combat stress,” she says. “That can be walking, gardening, reading, painting, or whatever else leads to more relaxation in general. Even if it’s just five minutes, set aside time for yourself and do something you truly enjoy.”

3. Focus on physical activity.

ALTHOUGH THE IMMUNE system appreciates rest and sleep, it also recharges thanks to regular exercise. Even just taking a five- to 10-minute walk every day can be beneficial for helping keep your immune system strong—and it helps the rest of your body, too.

Physical activity may help flush bacteria out of the lungs and airways, helping reduce your chance of illness, according to the National Institutes of Health (NIH). Exercise may cause beneficial changes in antibodies and white blood cells, which are both part of the immune system’s defense team. The NIH also states that the brief rise in body temperature when you’re active can help prevent bacteria from growing and becoming a threat. “You may see other advantages, too,” Acevedo says, “like stronger bones, more mobility, and better muscle tone.”

4. Keep drinking (water).

ADEQUATE HYDRATION IS essential for a strong immune system, Acevedo stresses. The older you get, the more vulnerable you are to dehydration, which research suggests can contribute to increased health risks. It can also throw off the body’s fluid balance, fuel inflammation, and even reduce the effectiveness of key immune defenders like T-cells and B-cells.

“Simply drinking more water is an easy way to support your immune system as well as other functions like the circulatory system,” says Acevedo. A good rule of thumb for adequate intake is to divide your body weight in half and drink that many ounces of water every day (ie, 100 ounces of water for a 200-pound man).

Staying hydrated also helps your other healthy habits pay off. When combined with good sleep, regular exercise, and stress reduction, it can help your body respond more effectively to infections, Acevedo says, helping to give your immune system support. Think of hydration as a foundation: It supports every other step toward healthy aging—and keeping what you got.
