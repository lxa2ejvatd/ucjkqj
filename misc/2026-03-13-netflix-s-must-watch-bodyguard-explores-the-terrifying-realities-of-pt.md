# Netflix's Must-Watch 'Bodyguard' Explores the Terrifying Realities of PTSD

In case you’ve been wondering what Robb Stark has been up to lately (RIP Robb, you were too pretty for Westeros), he’s in a TV series on Netflix called Bodyguard, and it’s actually one of the best things you can find on the streaming service right now.

Former Game of Thrones actor Richard Madden stars in the six-part British show—which originally aired on the BBC—as Sergeant David Budd, an Afghanistan War veteran who’s been tasked with protecting Home Secretary Julia Montague (Keeley Hawes), an ambitious political figure who seems to have enemies on all sides. There’s yelling, chases, covert operations, bombings, assassination attempts, and steamy sex. It’s hard not to binge!

But those aren’t even close to the most interesting things about Bodyguard, which has a lot more basis in reality than your favorite Kevin Costner and Whitney Houston movie. Madden—who has had a more rewarding post-Thrones career than some of his castmates—gives a superb, psychologically layered performance as David Budd, a man who’s been broken by his combat experience in Afghanistan and returning home. He has PTSD that goes untreated and sends him back to his most nightmarish war experiences at the most inopportune moments, like when he's trying to save a life.

These jolts of panic are terrifying for viewers, too, interrupting the badass action and giving context for Budd’s paranoia and aggression. Anyone who knows a veteran suffering from PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder) or other trauma will likely see the signs.

"PTSD is the nervous system's response to an overwhelming event that it's not able to process or integrate," says Avi Klein, L.C.S.W., a psychotherapist in New York City. And while it doesn't have to involve war or even violence, the more severe the situation, the more likely one is to experience trauma.

There may be memories of what happened, but the brain isn't able to fully recognize them as such and move on from them. "We don't have a sense of coherence around it the way you do other life experience," Klein adds. "So it keeps violently inserting itself either in our dreams or in our day-to-day or when something reminds us of it. It's like part of our nervous system hasn’t accepted that the threat is over so the functions we have to keep us safe are sent into overdrive."

Budd is constantly motivated by danger, not least of all because of his job. Home Secretary Montague is a warmonger who refuses to apologize for bungled military interventions in the Middle East, and she wants to create a surveillance state with the RIPA legislation, which conflicts with everything Budd feels is right. “Only those intent on acts of violence have anything to fear,” Montague says in one especially chilly TV interview.

Budd replays it, brooding. He sees her as a stand-in for politicians who unnecessarily put him in harm’s way, damaged his body and mind, and still haven’t owned up to it. You can see it make him sweat, even as he dutifully shields her. Madden—who interestingly also played a man with anxiety in the comedy movie Ibiza—is at his finest in these moments, telling us everything about how his character got here without saying anything. It’s hard to blame Budd for being the man he’s become.

Klein has seen very similar reactions in patients with trauma. "I think what [the creators are] trying to get at is his experience that led to his PTSD has also sort of shaped his political views," Klein says of Budd. "Then when he has to encounter her political views, it triggers his PTSD because it brings up all the same feelings again... To me as a therapist, it's easier for someone to get angry at people who hurt him than to do the work on himself to heal."

"It's like part of our nervous system hasn’t accepted that the threat is over."

“Mate, you can’t beat it on your own,” a disillusioned old military friend tells Budd of his PTSD. But he refuses the help with a swig of beer and an “I’m good, thanks.” He's part of a staggering statistic: According to the National Institute of Mental Health, only about half of people with mental illness receive treatment.

It's sad, but perhaps no surprise, that this takes a devastating toll on Budd's relationship with his estranged wife. “Don’t start,” she tells him during one drunken late-night call as he attempts to win her back yet can't genuinely open up about what's affecting him.

"That's very common," Klein says of the characters' interaction. "You don't want to feel stuff, you shut down, and a lot of partners have a hard time when they can’t emotionally connect to their husband or wife. It's like the person you love isn’t there anymore."

Klein is quick to add that anyone who feels they may be suffering from trauma should seek the best treatment they can from someone specializing in psychotherapy for that particular issue. "Healing is definitely possible," he says. One Army veteran speaking to the BBC was also hopeful that Bodyguard's depiction of real, under-discussed psychological problems may help save lives.

Bodyguard is very entertaining as pure action spectacle. The show sets up red herrings and makes you keep guessing who exactly is trying to hunt down Montague and what fresh terror is around the corner. But what makes it stand out is its deepening of Budd’s inner life. Sadly, even people who haven’t served in the military, don’t have PTSD, and don’t chase snipers for a living can identify with parts of what he's going through. The way his anxiety and emotional trauma affect his daily life is all too relatable for the tens of millions of people dealing with mental illness each year in the US.
