THE HUMAN BODY can only go without oxygen for 4 minutes before there’s irreversible brain damage. Death follows 4 to 6 minutes later. Yet one 33-year-old man stayed alive for 48 hours without either of his lungs.

Surgeons devised an ingenious way to keep the man alive after removing his infected lungs. Documented in a recent article in journal Med, the radical approach could work as a potential option for others waiting on an organ transplant.

How This Man Kept Breathing Without Lungs

The 33-year-old man needed immediate surgery after developing a condition called acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). It happens when swelling of the lungs creates fluid buildup in small air sacs called the alveoli. This leaves the lung with less space to take in oxygen. The lack of air becomes life-threatening as less oxygen reaches the bloodstream and the organs.

ARDS came about after a severe case of the flu. The man’s lungs continued to worsen after he developed bacterial pneumonia as well. Multiple organ failure was likely imminent. Along with his lungs, his heart and kidneys were rapidly declining.

“He was critically ill. His heart stopped as soon as he arrived. We had to perform CPR,” says Ankit Bharat, MBBS, lead author and chief of thoracic surgery at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine. “When the infection is so severe that the lungs are melting, they’re irrecoverably damaged. That’s when patients die.”

The man’s lungs could not be saved. His only chance of survival was in a double lung transplant—but there was another problem. The man’s body was too weak to undergo a lung transplant. At first glance, it seemed like they had two options. If they left the lungs, he would die. If they removed both lungs without an alternative way to oxygenate the body, it could lead to heart failure.

“The heart and lungs are intrinsically connected,” says Bharat. “When there are no lungs, how do you keep the patient alive?” So they devised a third option.

Bharat and his team created an artificial lung system to mimic the functions of a human lung. The system gave oxygen to the blood, removed carbon dioxide, and kept constant blood flow through the guy’s heart and body.

With oxygen pumping through the body, the doctors could now safely remove the infected lungs. There was an immediate improvement. His blood pressure stabilized, he recovered function in the kidney and heart, and the infection resolved.

The artificial lung gave the man’s body 48 hours to recover—and gave him enough time to wait for donor lungs to become available. The man underwent a double lung transplant and a 2-year follow-up shows he is still going strong with healthy lung function.

"This is a remarkable solution to the need to not just support lung function but also to remove the lungs to clear what otherwise is a difficult-to-treat and life-threatening infection," says Jimmy Johannes, MD, pulmonologist and critical care medicine specialist at MemorialCare Long Beach Medical Center who was not involved in the case. "Extracorporeal life support is already available to effectively treat refractory respiratory failure without having to remove the diseased lungs." With that said, Johannes says the operation shows it is possible to completely remove both lungs and safely keep a patient alive as they await surgery.

A Creative Solution Could Become Standard Practice One Day

“Conventionally, lung transplant is reserved for patients who have chronic conditions like interstitial lung disease or cystic fibrosis,” Bharat explains. “Currently, people think if you get severe ARDS, you keep supporting them and ultimately the lungs will get better.” Bharat, however, says the current report is proof this isn’t always the case.

When looking at the man’s original lungs at a molecular level, the doctors found extensive scarring and immune damage. In other words, they confirmed that the man’s lungs were never going to get better on their own.

“For the first time, biologically, we are giving molecular proof that some patients will need a double lung transplant, otherwise they will not survive,” says Bharat.

Johannes says the application would be limited to rare situations where removing the native diseased lungs several days before transplantation is needed. Though Bharat has hope that this will become more of a standardized approach to keep people alive while they are waiting for new lungs.
