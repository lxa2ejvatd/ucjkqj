What is Foot Drop? When Diabetes Affects Your Walking Gait

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Foot drop is the inability to lift the front of your foot, causing a "steppage" gait that increases your risk of tripping and falling. In people with diabetes, foot drop commonly develops when chronically high blood sugar damages peripheral nerves—particularly the peroneal nerve—resulting in weakness of the muscles that lift the foot.

Key facts about diabetic foot drop:

Cause: Peripheral neuropathy from prolonged high blood glucose

Main symptom: Difficulty lifting the front of the foot while walking

Primary risk: Falls and injury due to altered gait

Management: Blood sugar control, physical therapy, bracing (AFO), and sometimes surgery

Because foot drop can also signal other nerve or spine conditions, identifying the underlying cause early is essential to preventing permanent nerve damage. What Is Foot Drop?

Foot drop isn't a disease itself, but a sign of an underlying problem with the nerves or muscles that lift your foot. In people with diabetes, it most often happens because high blood sugar injures peripheral nerves.

Key features of foot drop:

Inability to dorsiflex (lift) the front of the foot

"Slap" or "steppage" gait: foot may drag or slap the ground

Risk of tripping, falling, or stumbling

May affect one side (unilateral) or both sides (bilateral)

How Diabetes Leads to Foot Drop

Diabetes can cause nerve damage—known as diabetic neuropathy—when high blood glucose levels injure nerve fibers over time. While the most common form is distal symmetric polyneuropathy (affecting toes and feet first), more focal neuropathies can affect specific nerves, including:

Common peroneal nerve (around the knee): Controls muscles that lift the foot

Sciatic nerve (in the hip/buttock area): Splits into the peroneal and tibial nerves

When these nerves are compressed, stretched, or otherwise damaged, the signals telling your muscles to lift your foot weaken or stop, resulting in foot drop.

Recognizing Symptoms

Foot drop can develop gradually or suddenly. Watch for:

Toe dragging when taking a step

High-stepping gait: lifting the knee higher than usual to clear the foot

Numbness, tingling, or burning in the top of the foot or outer part of the leg

Weakness when trying to flex the ankle upward

Pain around the shin or knee (less common)

If you have diabetes, these signs could mean your blood sugar is affecting the nerves in your leg.

Diagnosing Foot Drop in Diabetes

A healthcare provider will take a thorough history and do a physical exam. They may check:

Muscle strength in your foot and ankle

Sensation (light touch, vibration) in the leg and foot

Reflexes at the knee and ankle

Additional tests may include:

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG) to pinpoint nerve damage

Treatment Options

Managing foot drop in the context of diabetes involves both controlling blood sugar and addressing the nerve injury directly.

Blood Sugar Control

Aim for HbA1c targets your doctor sets (usually below 7% for many adults)

Monitor glucose regularly and adjust diet or medications as needed

Physical Therapy

Exercises to strengthen ankle dorsiflexors (e.g., toe raises)

Gait training and balance exercises to reduce fall risk

Stretching to prevent stiffness in calf muscles

Bracing and Orthotics

Ankle-Foot Orthosis (AFO) brace holds the ankle at a 90° angle

Over-the-counter or custom shoe inserts improve stability

Medications

Pain relievers for neuropathic pain (e.g., duloxetine, pregabalin)

Supplements if vitamin deficiencies (B12, folate) are present

Preventing Foot Drop and Falls

Taking steps to protect your nerves and maintain strength can reduce your risk:

Keep blood sugar within target range

Rotate positions if sitting or crossing legs for long periods

Avoid tight casts or compression around the knee

Exercise regularly: walking, swimming, cycling

Inspect feet daily for cuts, sores, or infections

Wear supportive, well-fitting shoes

Living with Foot Drop and Diabetes

Foot drop can feel alarming, but with the right plan, you can manage symptoms and stay active.

• Celebrate small gains: even slight improvements in ankle strength help• Build support: work with physical therapists, diabetes educators, and podiatrists• Stay safe: clear home walkways, use nightlights, remove loose rugs• Monitor changes: report new numbness, pain, or weakness to your care team

When to Seek Medical Help

Foot drop can sometimes signal a more serious problem, especially if it appears suddenly or with other concerning signs. Contact a doctor right away if you experience:

Sudden inability to move your foot or ankle

Severe pain in the back, leg, or foot accompanied by weakness

Signs of infection on the foot: redness, swelling, warmth, drainage

Uncontrolled blood sugar levels despite treatment

Frequent falls or injuries

Foot drop in diabetes isn't something to ignore. Early diagnosis and treatment can prevent complications and help you maintain independence.
