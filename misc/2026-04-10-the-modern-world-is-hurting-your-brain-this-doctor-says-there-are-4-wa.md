# The Modern World Is Hurting Your Brain. This Doctor Says There Are 4 Ways to Fix It.

Our product picks are editor-tested, expert-approved. We may earn a commission through links on our site. Why Trust Us?

I’VE BEEN PRACTICING psychiatry for more than 20 years, and I can’t help but notice significant changes in my patients from when I started my career. The modern world is taking its toll on the human brain. We’re tethered to our phones and social media yet feel more isolated and disconnected than ever. We’re experiencing dramatic deficits in nature and engagement—both things known to help our brains thrive. We’re stressed to our breaking points, at work and at home. Our lives are filled with natural and man-made toxins, worming their way into us with every breath we take.

It’s affecting our mental health. Even people who don’t meet the criteria for having depression or anxiety tell me they just don’t feel good. Something is missing from their lives, but they can’t quite figure out what it is or what to do about it. The way to start: Just as you would in any process where you’re looking for a solution, define the problem. As psychiatrists, we call it self-awareness, but it’s the same thing. Build it with these steps.

Listen to What You’re Really Saying

OFTEN, PATIENTS WHO have just shared something very self-aware will follow it with the phrase “I don’t know.” Take, for example, a patient I call Charlotte. She started pouring herself a glass of wine while making dinner for her family during the pandemic. Over time, it became two, and two became three. She was shocked by how much alcohol she had been consuming—and continued to consume without a second thought. It was affecting her sleep. It was affecting her mood. Yet she couldn’t quite figure out how to lessen her intake.

When she came to see me, her first words were: “I think I may be drinking too much, I don’t know.” Therapists refer to this habit as “disavowal.” Meaning, we are emptying out important knowledge we have—and are a little bit uncomfortable as we do so. If you catch yourself doing this, try swapping out the “don’t” in “I don’t know” for “very clearly” and see how you feel about the preceding statement.

When I asked Charlotte to repeat her statement as “I think I may be drinking too much, I very clearly know,” it resonated. Her subconscious was trying to tell her something. Maybe yours is, too.

Also, make self-awareness a judgment-free space. Many activities we do, such as engaging in a nightly booze ritual, come about not because we are “bad” or “unhealthy.” They develop because we’re not paying attention as they transform into automatic habits. When we’re more self-aware, we can pay attention—and understand what it will take to change them. That moves us into a space where we can foster both the desire and the resources to heal our brains.

Focus on the What

REFRAMING THE QUESTIONS you ask yourself can provide information to help you make changes. Research from Tasha Eurich, PhD, an organizational psychologist who studies self-awareness, suggests you can bolster your self-awareness when you focus on the what instead of the why.

Think about it. Far too often, our introspection is nothing more than a why question. Why did I say that? Why can’t I go to bed at a reasonable hour? Unfortunately, when we frame it this way, we tend to focus on the negatives. I said that because I was angry. I can’t go to bed at a reasonable hour because I’m so stressed out at work. None of those answers give you much more than continued gloomy feelings. But when you start asking yourself what questions, you can begin thinking about potential solutions to whatever may be ailing you.

Consider the bedtime question. Instead of asking yourself why, think about what may be happening that’s keeping you from sleep. Sure, work may be stressing you out right now. But that does not help you address the matter at hand. Instead, imagine what’s happening in your personal world each night that may be contributing. Are you doomscrolling when you get into bed? Mindlessly accepting Netflix’s invitation to watch the next episode? If that’s your what, putting a hard stop on screen time an hour before bed might help.

When you focus on the what instead of the why, you become more open to learning about your habits and finding ways to manage them—and bring yourself more peace and well-being.

Take More Responsibility

PEOPLE OFTEN COMPLAIN about institutions or other people (as in: The client’s not clear; the medical establishment won’t listen). This lets us externalize the issue at hand and put the blame on others. The trouble is that this is incredibly disempowering. If the problem lies with those other people, we lack the ability to change it.

One patient we’ll call Tim was always enraged that his boss would criticize his projects and give him work that he thought was insultingly below his level. There are plenty of bad bosses, but when this kept happening in job after job, we looked deeper and discovered that his self-esteem was dependent on these people he didn’t even like that much. When he learned that a project was just a project, and that a few criticisms weren’t going to end his career, he felt more secure and relaxed.

When you heighten your awareness around a tendency to externalize problems, you give yourself more control (you can ask the client to explain, or find another doctor), and it puts you on a path to better mental fitness.

See Who’s in Your Corner

PLENTY OF RESEARCH shows that having a diverse web of social connections is critical to your mental well-being. But we’re often unaware of how big or small our social networks are. I do this three-minute connection inventory with all my patients. Jot down your connections, categorizing them as romantic partner(s)/spouse, immediate family, extended family, friends, neighbors, acquaintances, colleagues, role models, mentors, classmates, spiritual community members, volunteer community members, and any other category relevant to you. Taking inventory helps you become aware of where you spend most of your social capital and where you might be able to create other connections.

Because the modern brain is up against some new challenges, I’ve shifted my approach as a mental health clinician to help people both recover their mental health and also optimize their mental fitness. Putting in effort to increase your self-awareness, regain personal agency, and foster more layers of connection is needed today more than ever. Modern times call for modern mental fitness.
