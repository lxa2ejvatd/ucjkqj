How dangerous is stopping aspirin suddenly?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Suddenly stopping aspirin can be dangerous. Research shows that abruptly discontinuing low-dose aspirin—especially for people using it to prevent heart attacks or strokes—can raise the risk of serious cardiovascular events, including heart attack, stroke, and blood clots, within days of stopping.

Why is stopping aspirin risky? Aspirin works by preventing platelets from clumping together. When you stop taking it, a "rebound effect" can occur, making your blood more prone to clotting than before you started. This is especially concerning for people with a history of heart disease, stents, or prior stroke.

What should you do? Never stop aspirin without first talking to your doctor. If you're experiencing side effects or considering stopping, a medical professional can help you weigh the risks and transition safely.

Because symptoms like chest discomfort, unusual bruising, stomach pain, or shortness of breath can signal something serious, it's important to identify what's actually going on before making any changes to your medication.
