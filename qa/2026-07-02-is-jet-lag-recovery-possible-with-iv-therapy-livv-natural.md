Understanding jet lag

What’s jet lag? It’s the disruptive outcome of traveling a long distance quickly. It occurs when you traverse two or more time zones by plane.

Your body’s “internal clock” or circadian sleep rhythms change abruptly. You feel out of sorts and may experience sleep problems.

Each time you travel, your circadian rhythm needs ample time to adjust. It has to register your new sleep and wake patterns at your destination.

Remember, the circadian rhythm regulates your sleep-wake cycles. Disrupting it causes temporary sleep challenges.

Jet lag also arises from sitting on a plane for a long time. Insufficient oxygen and lower air pressure inside the plane cabin are also causes. Warm temperatures and low humidity inside the airplane can lead to dehydration.

The common symptoms of jet lag include:

Difficulty sleeping (insomnia)

Headaches

Daytime drowsiness

Loss of focus or concentration

Extreme fatigue

Stomach upsets

Not feeling like yourself

Mood swings (irritability)

These symptoms typically disappear on their own within days. Seek medical intervention if your body is taking longer than usual to adjust.

What increases the severity of jet lag? Some factors include:

The number of time zones crossed. Traversing multiple time zones makes it more difficult for the body to adjust. Each additional time zone complicates the synchronization of circadian rhythms.

Travel direction. Flying eastward could cause more severe jet lag due to a shorter day. Going westward allows for a longer day, making it easier for the body to adjust. Your body adapts better to staying up late than sleeping earlier than usual.

Travel pattern and frequency. Are you a frequent long-haul traveler? Inadequate recovery time can intensify your jet lag. Irregular flying patterns cause perennial changes in time zones. It’s hard for your body to establish a consistent circadian rhythm.

Age and individual adaptability. Older folks may have longer recovery periods than younger travelers.

Upsetting the circadian rhythm can affect your overall well-being. You may lose your ability to stay alert, sleep, digest food, or perform different tasks. These rhythms define your body’s activity pattern based on a 24-hour day.

Besides digestion, circadian rhythms also affect your body temperature and hormones. The brain guides your body to set these rhythms naturally. Sometimes, external factors like light impact the rhythms.

For instance, light entering your eyes triggers messaging disruption. Your cells tell the brain to stop producing melatonin. This hormone helps you sleep. Insufficient melatonin causes sleep disturbances.

The science behind IV therapy

IV therapy directly administers medications, fluids, or nutrients into the bloodstream. The delivery occurs via a vein for quick absorption and distribution across the body. It’s one of the most common procedures that collaborates with the circulatory system.

Doctors administer this therapy through a specialized IV line. This unique mechanism bypasses the digestive system. The shorter route allows the body to receive more fluids per unit of time. The amount and rate of fluid delivered are controllable, optimizing results.

The key components of an IV line include a:

Small plastic tubing (cannula) for the vein

Needle

Tiny plastic tubing for the bag of fluid

During your procedure, the doctor inserts the cannula into your vein. The most common placement area is the crook of the arm. They then attach another tube connected to an IV bag with the needed fluids. Once the setup is complete, the fluid starts dripping into the vein.

Typically, IV therapy has a faster onset of action than oral administration. It’s more suitable for acute conditions.

DIY strategies for jet lag prevention

How can you brace for and combat jet lag hassle-free before and during travel? Consider these top tips:

Adjust your sleep and meal times gradually as you near the travel date.

Get used to the destination’s time zone during the flight.

Plan your journey early.

Prepare well for any stopover to be cozy enough.

Hydrate enough with fruit juice or water rather than tea or coffee.

Make early arrangements at the destination.

Once you arrive, consider taking a shower, a non-alcoholic drink, or a brief nap.

Why are optimized hydration and nutrition essential to minimize jet lag? Long-haul traveling typically causes dehydration, which may induce fatigue. Proper hydration before and after your flight could combat exhaustion. It might also alleviate jet lag symptoms like headaches.

Carry an empty water bottle past airport security and fill it up later. You can also buy water at the terminal or order it in-flight. Consume plenty of water even after your arrival. Avoid high-salt foods, caffeine, and alcohol before and during your flight. They may worsen dehydration.
