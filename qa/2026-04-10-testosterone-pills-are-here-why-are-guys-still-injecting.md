# Testosterone Pills Are Here. Why Are Guys Still Injecting?

Irwin Goldstein, MD, director of San Diego Sexual Medicine, takes testosterone replacement therapy, just like many of his patients. Over more than two decades, he’s tried it all—shots, pellets, gels, creams, nasal sprays–but switched to pills a few years ago when a new crop of oral testosterone formulas hit the market. The first, Jatenzo, was approved by the FDA in 2019, followed by Tlando and Kyzatrex in 2022.

“When a person walks in my office, whatever testosterone they're on, I take them off it, and I put them on orals,” says Dr. Goldstein. Dr. Goldstein calls oral T “game changing” because it eliminates the need to stick a needle in your hip or rub gel into your skin, which could transfer to anyone you touch.

“All you have to do is open your mouth and swallow,” he says.

Research suggests that testosterone replacement therapy is growing in popularity, especially in men ages 35 to 54. However, oral T isn’t prescribed as often as the most popular forms: injections and gels.

“The orals haven't sort of ‘made it’ in medicine,” says Dr. Goldstein. Here’s why, and what you need to know about your options.

The Fall and Slow Rebirth of T Pills

The first oral testosterone formula, methyltestosterone, was invented in the 1930s. This medication caused liver disease, so it never took off.

“It became embedded in the history of testosterone therapy: Do not use orals,” says Dr. Goldstein.

In the decades that followed, testosterone injections and topicals became the standard, widely accepted by doctors and covered by insurance companies. Today, millions of men use some form of testosterone replacement therapy to restore testosterone, a sex hormone that decreases by 1 percent per year after age 30. Testosterone replacement therapy is often recommended when men have classic low T symptoms, such as low libido, fatigue, irritability, and low energy, along with blood levels of testosterone under 300 nanograms per deciliter (ng/dL), according to the American Urological Association.

The new generation of T pills, Jatenzo, Tlando, and Kyzatrex, don’t hurt your liver like the old testosterone medications. They’re made with testosterone undecanoate instead of methyltestosterone and metabolized by the lymphatic system instead of the liver.

How the Different Forms of T Work

T pills, shots, gels, and other forms all increase testosterone in slightly different ways.

Pills

With pills, you usually take one in the morning and one at night. The downside is that you must remember to take them. The upside is they’re convenient and provide a steady dose of T throughout the day. There’s also something else that’s unique about the pill form of testosterone. While raising T, it also reduces sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG). This protein binds to testosterone and carries it through your bloodstream.

Here’s why that matters: If your SHBG is high, your T is bound up and unavailable, says Dr. Goldstein. That means there’s less “free testosterone” in your system. “You could have a high serum testosterone [the total level of testosterone in your blood], but if you have a low free testosterone, you basically have a low testosterone state,” he says. When oral T medication increases your T and reduces SHBG, your free T goes up, so you can feel more of the benefits of testosterone, such as building muscle and increasing libido.

Injections

If you use testosterone shots, or intramuscular injections, you give yourself a shot once every week or two. Your T spikes by days two through four, then tapers off as you approach the next dose. There’s also a newer subcutaneous shot (Xyosted) that comes in an autoinjector pen and provides a steadier increase in T than old-school shots.

Gels, Patches and Pellets

If you go the topical gel route, you apply testosterone-containing gel to your back once a day. One downside is that topical testosterone can rub off onto other people when you hug them or transfer to items that others touch, like gym mats, says Dr. Goldstein. Some kids exposed to these products have experienced early puberty. T patches can get around that problem, but they can cause rashes in some users. Another option is a testosterone pellet inserted under the skin of your butt cheek every four months by a doctor.

“Some people don't like to take pills every day, and they're very happy coming in once every four months, getting these pellets,” says Stanton Honig, MD, Professor of Urology and Chief of Reproductive and Sexual Medicine in the Department of Urology at Yale University School of Medicine. “Other people don't like shots. Other people don't like rubbing gels on, so there are pluses and minuses of each one of these, but the orals are actually working really well.”

The Risks of Oral T

Oral T doesn’t appear to be any riskier than other forms. It’s only a no-go if you’re allergic to one of its inactive ingredients—that’s true with the other forms, too—or if you’re not a candidate for testosterone replacement therapy in the first place. For example, men who are trying to conceive children should avoid it.

“We see a lot of young guys coming in who don't know that it can affect their fertility,” says Dr. Honig. “Testosterone shuts off sperm production.” Suppose you’re trying to become a dad but have low T. In that case, doctors can recommend alternatives, such as clomiphene citrate or human chorionic gonadotropin, instead. They can increase your T levels enough to improve symptoms while preserving your sperm production.

Fortunately, the old-school concerns about the potential heart risks of testosterone replacement therapy have been put to rest. In February 2025, the FDA removed the boxed warning on testosterone products that previously advised users about an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. This change came after a large 2023 study showed no increased risk of cardiovascular death, heart attacks, or strokes in men who took testosterone for an average of 22 months.

For some guys, testosterone can raise blood pressure by a few points. “The chances of a man with high blood pressure, particularly if it's not perfectly well controlled, getting an elevation in blood pressure on testosterone is definitely greater than a man who has got completely normal blood pressure and has no blood pressure medications,” says William B. White, M.D., professor emeritus of the Calhoun Cardiology Center at the University of Connecticut School of Medicine. If you have a history of high BP, he recommends having your doctor check it four to six weeks after starting testosterone therapy.

Why Your Doc Might Not Have Suggested Pills

Despite the benefits of oral T, many primary care doctors and urologists aren’t prescribing it. One reason: The cost.

“Historically, the rub-on gels and intramuscular testosterone cypionate are the ones that are usually covered by insurance,” says Dr. Honig.

The older gels and shots come in inexpensive generic versions. The new pills, which are still protected by patents, can cost patients $150 to $175 per month, Dr. Honig says.

“Some insurances were covering it for a while, but in the last six months, we've had more problems getting the oral testosterone covered because some of them have backed off,” says Dr. Honig. “A lot of times, you actually have to fail the gels, or you have to fail the intramuscular shots [in order to get the pills].”

Until insurance covers these pills, many men will use gels or injections to save money, not because they like using them, says Dr. Goldstein.

Dr. Goldstein also suspects that COVID might have slowed the adoption of oral T. Usually, when companies launch new drugs, they send an army of salespeople to doctors’ offices to educate them about the product. COVID might have shut that down, leaving doctors unaware of these pills and how they differ from the liver-damaging T pills of the last century. Plus, the companies behind these products are small, not household-name pharma companies with seemingly infinite marketing budgets. The company that commercialized Jatenzo, Clarus Therapeutics Holdings, went bankrupt and sold the brand—and its flagship product—in 2022 in part due to poor sales.

Dr. Goldstein also points to another factor. Some men (and doctors) take pride in the numbers on their blood tests, rather than symptom improvements. They fixate on staying above a certain threshold, such as 800 or 1,000 ng/dL of testosterone. These guys might prefer shots, which lead to quicker, more drastic T spikes on lab reports, over the less dramatic steady improvements seen with pills.

How do you decide which T to try?

“The testosterone that you take is really based on the delivery system that's going to work best for you,” says Dr. Honig.

If patients meet the prescribing criteria–low T and low T symptoms with no fertility plans–Dr. Honig lays out the options. “I tell them the cost issues, risks, and benefits of each one, and then I let them decide what they want to do,” he says.
