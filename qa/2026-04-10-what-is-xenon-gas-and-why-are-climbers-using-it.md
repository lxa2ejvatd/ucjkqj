# What Is Xenon Gas, and Why Are Climbers Using It?

IF YOU COULD scale Everest and back in less than a week, would you do it? That's exactly what a British expedition did, with the potential assistance of a secret ingredient: xenon gas.

Right now, ascending Everest generally takes six to ten weeks, as you spend time at high elevation to acclimatize to the oxygen-thin air. Skip that portion, and you run the risk of severe altitude sickness—symptoms of which can include headaches, nausea, weakness, brain swelling, and even death.

Xenon gas has been hypothesized to shorten that adaptation time, as well as improve performance high up on the mountain. In May, a British group with four special forces members, completed a charity summit of Everest in only three days with Furtenbach Adventures, a company that specializes in speed ascents supplementing with xenon. Their goal: achieve the fastest accent of Everest from London—there, to the summit, and back, in less than 7 days—in support of the bereaved children of fallen veterans.

Anthony 'Staz' Stazicker, 41, Co-Founder of ThruDark, was part of the group that did it. About 10 weeks prior to their quest, the group began sleeping in low-oxygen conditions with a hypoxic tent placed around their beds. They trained for upwards of 12 hours a week through a mixture of strength training, conditioning, cardio, and "time on feet," such as hiking, Stazicker says. For a few hours a week, they would also train with a mask on that lowered their oxygen levels.

None of that is uncommon in preparing for an Everest summit. What is uncommon, is what they did next.

A few weeks before the expedition, the crew gathered in Germany to begin testing with xenon gas under the supervision of anesthesiologist Michael Fries. There, they underwent a series of medical tests before and after the ascent. Fries' team led a bit of an experiment on these men—the hypothesis being that xenon raises the amount of red blood cells in the body, which promotes oxygen transport to the muscles. They were put through a series of medical tests before and after the ascent, as well as monitoring during the climb itself. There, they were exposed to a specialized mixture of xenon and oxygen.

"For me, I felt nothing other than a tiny bit lightheaded and maybe a little drunk. And, immediately as I came off the gas, I'm back to normal," Stazicker says.

Though there is very little research done, a small handful of climbers have used this tactic as a way to lower both time spent on the mountain, as well as to feel better while exercising under such harsh conditions. Many think it could improve the safety of the climb as mountaineers spend less time in the unforgiving terrain, and limit environmental impact.

"Clearly there needs to be some more research, more peer-reviewed science behind using xenon in climbing. I was just kind of a bit of a guinea pig," Stazicker says. "But to me, if it can make the climb safer for anybody, then we should at least think about using it."

Of course, the tactic has drawn controversy in the sport of mountaineering. Some claim that the introduction of the gas can be seen as a type of doping (xenon has been on the World Anti-Doping Agency's list of banned substances since 2014). It's use could also limit the amount of time spent on Everest—and while that might be a positive for safety and environmental impact, it could also decrease the tourism economy in Nepal, which is heavily relied on by locals and sherpas.

What is xenon gas?

XENON GAS IS a rare colorless, odorless gas that is present in the Earth's atmosphere in very small amounts. It has several traditional uses, like in certain lighting apparatuses including vehicle lights and movie projectors.

In the medical field, xenon "has been used as an anesthetic before surgeries, but it's not really used a whole lot today," says William Cornwell, M.D., director of Sports Cardiology at the University of Colorado, who has participated in research studies on the use of xenon gas in sport. It's used less often now because we've discovered better agents, he says.

When too much is inhaled at a high concentration, you could run the risk of asphyxiation. Symptoms include dizziness, shortness of breath, loss of consciousness, nausea, and death.

Can xenon gas really help high summit climbs?

RESEARCHERS AREN'T CONVINCED.

Xenon, when used in anesthesia, "stimulates a variety of different biologic responses. One of those is through the hypoxia [a condition where the body is subjected to lower levels of oxygen] response pathway," says Benjamin D. Levine, M.D., director of the Institute for Exercise and Environmental Medicine, who has led extensive research on the health effects of xenon, and is an expert in sports cardiology. "It raises erythropoietin, the hormone controlled by the kidneys that controls red blood cells. So, in theory, at sea level, raising erythropoietin could raise the red blood cells and improve oxygen transport."

Conversations around using xenon for sport have been circulating since the Sochi Olympics, when news broke about Russian athletes potentially doping with xenon. However, when tested, evidence wasn't found that it assists in athletic performance.

Studies have tested xenon doping on athletic performance, and have ultimately found it has no effect. "In realistic doses, for realistic time periods, in realistic people, it just doesn't improve athletic performance," Cornwell says.

Those findings are more proven at high altitudes.

"We know already, from very well done studies, that once you get above about 4000 meters, an [increased amount of red blood cells] doesn't improve climbing or exercise performance," Levine says. For reference, Everest is about 8,848 meters high. Once you get to extreme hypoxic environments, the gradient of oxygen from the blood into the muscles gets so low that it doesn't matter how many red blood cells you have.

The basis of success of this mission more likely lies in the amount of oxygen the team has access to, as well as an attribution to the climber's great athletic abilities and rigorous training, Cornwell says. Supplemental oxygen was supplied at 8 liters per minute during the climb, which undoubtedly contributed to their ability to function during extreme hypoxia. The combination of the use of supplemental oxygen, the pre-travel use of hypoxic tents, and the climber's seriously impressive physical fitness, all makes it difficult to know whether xenon gas had any impact at all on the success of the trek.

In this case, then, there's really no way to know if the xenon did anything—other than create a stir in the climbing world.
