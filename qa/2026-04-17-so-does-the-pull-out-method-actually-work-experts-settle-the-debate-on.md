SPEED DEMONS KNOW pulling out as a merging strategy, while politicians use it synonymously with dropping out. But in the sexicon—yes, that’s sex lexicon—pulling out refers to an effective birth control strategy.

For the uninitiated, the pull-out method hinges on the penis-owner pulling their pecker out of their partner before ejaculating. Estimates from one small August 2025 study suggest that 62 percent of sexually active people have utilized the pull-out method as their primary form of birth control at some point in their lives.

Certainly, the convenience and low cost of this method are obvious. But “it’s not a reliable way to prevent unwanted pregnancy,” says board-certified urologist and female pelvic medicine specialist Michael Ingber, MD, with The Center for Specialized Women's Health in New Jersey. Sorry to burst your bubble, but “data suggests that it’s just about 78 percent effective, which means about 22 couples per 100 will become pregnant through utilizing this method per year.” Hey, there’s a reason it’s also called the pull and pray method.

Ultimately, choosing the right birth control is a personal decision, but if avoiding pregnancy is a top priority, you’d be wise to understand the risks of using the pull-out method. Here’s what to know:

What is the pull-out method?

The pull-out method is a risky contraceptive option that involves a penis-owner removing—or attempting to remove—their penis from a vagina owner before ejaculation, explains Dr. Ingber.

Also known as withdrawal, coitus interruptus, and the retreat method, the premise behind pulling out is that semen doesn’t enter the vagina, and therefore pregnancy can’t occur. The problem? That assumption doesn’t fully hold up—thanks to factors like pre-ejaculate and imperfect timing—which is why it fails about 22 percent of the time with typical use, says women’s fertility expert Sophia Yen, MD, MPH, the founder of Pandia Health, an online birth control provider.

How effective is the pull-out method?

“For pregnancy to occur, sperm must somehow make their way into the vagina, travel through the cervix, and eventually fertilize an egg,” says Dr. Ingber. Usually, this happens during penis-in-vaginal intercourse, which is what’s relevant for conversations about the effectiveness of the pull-out method, but this can also take place through assisted methods like intrauterine insemination (IUI) or in vitro fertilization (IVF).

With that, the pull-out method can fail and result in an unwanted pregnancy anytime sperm is released from the penis prior to the, well, pulling out, he says.

That’s because, when ejaculation occurs from individuals who do not have a vasectomy, the penis releases a mix of seminal fluid and millions of sperm. If you don’t withdraw in time, or you begin ejaculating before pulling out, sperm can enter the vagina and fertilize an egg. “Even a very small amount of semen contains millions of sperm,” Dr. Ingber says.

Withdrawal requires a marriage of body awareness and self-control that most people don’t have consistently. “Sexual pleasure can make it difficult to stop, so anything that changes control, like alcohol, can make it more difficult to time pulling out,” says board-certified urologist, Peter Schlegel, MD, FACS, an internationally acclaimed expert in the treatment of male fertility and advisor with Ro, a sexual health pharmaceutical and supplement company. “Those with issues with premature ejaculation, where even limited stimulation can bring on a climax, may have an especially difficult time with pulling out on time," he notes.

Even if you time it perfectly and pull out before orgasm, there’s still one more risk factor: pre-cum. Pre-ejaculate fluid is produced by the Cowper’s glands and usually doesn’t contain sperm, says Dr. Ingber. But “if there are sperm still in the urethra from a prior ejaculation, they can mix with the pre-cum and make their way into the vagina,” he says. Some studies suggest that roughly 12 percent of pre-ejaculate samples contain enough sperm to cause pregnancy.

Why do people use withdrawal as birth control?

In the United States, fewer than half of states require sex ed to be medically accurate, and many still allow or even mandate abstinence-only curricula. Meaning, a lot of people grow up with incomplete or misleading information about birth control, and may not realize just how unreliable the pull-out method actually is.

Others turn to the pull-out method as their sole form of birth control because they crave the intimacy—or rumored intimacy—of skin-on-skin, which is not possible with external (penis) or internal (vaginal) condoms. Indeed, one small Contraception study found that perceived intimacy and pleasure were common reasons people chose withdrawal. Others fall back on it when they’ve run out of condoms, or if a partner has a latex allergy and non-latex or lambskin options aren’t available.

Some people, however, use it as a secondary method alongside fertility awareness–based methods (FABMs). The quick and dirty of FABMs—also known as ovulation tracking or cycle—is that people with ovaries can only get pregnant when there is an egg hanging out in the fallopian tube, which is where fertilization takes place. One of the ovaries releases just one egg during ovulation, which lives in the fallopian tubes for less than 24 hours after it’s released.

The idea is that if a person with ovaries can pinpoint when they are ovulating—using basal temperature tracking, ovulation urine tracking, and/or cervical mucus monitoring—they can avoid vaginal intercourse during ovulation days, plus five before and after. “Sperm can live up to 5 days in another body, so you need to avoid 5 days before and 5 days after ovulation to be safe,” explains Dr. Yen.

The catch is that fertility tracking is an imperfect science. “Ovulation usually occurs 14 days after the first day of the last period,” says Dr. Yen, but it can be pushed out farther due to stress, illness, thyroid issues, or other hormonal changes. “And most ovulation tests only confirm when you’re already ovulating, which is basically like finding out you should have been avoiding sex since five days ago,” she says. With that, even as a secondary method, it’s still far from foolproof.

Alternatives to the Pull-Out Method

If you and your partner(s) don’t want to become pregnant—or haven’t yet talked through your comfort with risk—it’s smart to look beyond withdrawal. As Dr. Ingber puts it, “At the end of the day, withdrawal is not a reliable form of birth control.”

With perfect use, external condoms are 98 percent effective, and with typical use, they’re around 87 percent effective, according to Planned Parenthood. “Condoms have the added benefit of reducing the risk of transmitting or acquiring sexually transmitted infections, which neither the pull-out method nor most other birth control options do,” Dr. Ingber says.

Vasectomy is another option if you know you don’t want to become a parent, now or in the future. This minor surgical procedure involves cutting or sealing the vas deferens, the tubes that carry sperm out of the testicles. “Vasectomy is being increasingly requested by men, including those who have not had children previously,” says Schlegel. Since the 2022 Dobbs decision that overturned Roe v. Wade, demand for vasectomies has nearly doubled, he says.

That said, vasectomy is intended as permanent contraception. While reversals exist, they’re not always offered to men who haven’t had children, and success isn’t guaranteed. Estimates suggest reversal works in only 30 to 90 percent of cases, depending on surgical technique and how much time has passed. “Vasectomy is, of course, a surgical procedure,” says Dr. Schlegel. “Although very safe, it’s not as simple as using a condom.” Fair.

If your partner has a uterus and is open to it being the one in charge of pregnancy prevention, there are other birth control options. Hormonal birth control pills, patches, and rings can be incredibly effective (over 99) if taken regularly and properly, says Dr. Yen. Longer-term methods are even more reliable. IUDs (intrauterine devices) and the implant are all more than 99 percent effective and can last anywhere from three to ten years, depending on the type, she says.

Should you really rely on the pull-out method?

Ultimately, it depends on what kind of risk profile you and your partner(s) are comfortable with. “Pull-out is a gamble, so if you're a gambling guy, go for it,” says Dr. Ingber. “Just be ready for the roles and responsibilities that come with parenting,” should your partner decide to move forward with a pregnancy. Indeed, it’s prudent to have a conversation (or five) about how you’ll handle it emotionally, financially, and logistically if your partner gets pregnant and how you’d both navigate that decision together.

“If, however, you’re not ready to be a parent, it’s best to use another contraceptive method,” says Dr. Ingber. As a penis-owner, the two options currently available for you are condoms or a vasectomy. The best way to know if you’re a candidate for the latter is to talk to a knowledgeable healthcare provider, such as your primary physician, male fertility specialist, or urologist.

If you opt for the former, keep in mind that not all condoms are created—or sized—equally. If you’ve tried one brand or style and didn’t like the feel, don’t write them off completely. “There are ultra-thin condoms, different fits, and brands designed for a more ‘natural’ feel,” says Dr. Ingber. It may take some trial and error to find the right one, but most guys would agree it’s worth it in the name of that sweet, sweet high sensation, super safe combo.
