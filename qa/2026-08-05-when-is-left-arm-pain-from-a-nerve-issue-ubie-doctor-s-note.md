When is left arm pain from a nerve issue?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Left arm pain often signals a nerve problem when it feels burning, shooting, electric, or like "pins and needles," travels a clear path from the neck or shoulder into the hand, and comes with numbness or specific muscle weakness. Nerve-related pain is typically triggered or worsened by certain neck, elbow, or wrist positions. However, it's critical to rule out look-alikes such as heart-related pain or muscle and joint injuries. Seek emergency care immediately for red flags like sudden crushing chest pain, jaw or shoulder radiation, shortness of breath, or rapidly worsening weakness.

Explanation

Left arm pain can arise from many sources—muscles, joints, blood vessels…and nerves. Nerve-related pain often has a distinctive quality and pattern. Understanding when left arm pain stems from a nerve issue can help you get the right treatment and avoid unnecessary worry.

How to Recognize Nerve-Related Left Arm Pain

Pain Quality

Shooting, burning, electric-shock or "pins and needles" sensations

Often follows a nerve pathway rather than spreading diffusely

Associated Neurologic Signs

Numbness or tingling in a specific distribution (dermatome or nerve territory)

Muscle weakness: trouble gripping, lifting, extending wrist

Changes in reflexes (e.g., decreased biceps or triceps reflex)

Movement and Position Triggers

Cervical radiculopathy pain is often aggravated by neck movements (Spurling's test: tilting the head toward the painful side + light compression reproduces symptoms)

Carpal tunnel pain may worsen with wrist flexion (Phalen's maneuver)

Ulnar neuropathy flares with elbow flexion

Difference from Other Causes of Left Arm Pain

Cardiac pain (angina/myocardial infarction) often feels like crushing pressure, heaviness or tightness, and may accompany shortness of breath, sweating, nausea.

Muscle strain feels achy, tender to touch, and worsens with local muscle use rather than following a nerve distribution.

Joint pain is localized to the shoulder, elbow or wrist joint and hurts with joint motion rather than specific nerve stretches.

Diagnostic Approach

Detailed History

Onset, quality, radiation, triggers

Any neck injury or repetitive stress (phone use, computer work)

Associated symptoms: headache, balance issues, systemic signs (fever, weight loss)

Physical Examination

Neurologic exam: sensation testing, muscle strength, reflexes

Special tests: Spurling's test, Tinel's sign at the wrist/elbow, Phalen's test

Imaging and Electrodiagnostics

MRI of the cervical spine to detect disc herniations or foraminal stenosis

X-rays to assess bone spurs or alignment issues

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG) to localize nerve compression and assess severity

Treatment Options

Conservative Management

Rest and activity modification: avoid positions that worsen pain

Physical therapy: cervical traction, posture training, nerve gliding exercises

Over-the-counter pain relievers: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) or acetaminophen

Interventional Therapies

Cervical epidural steroid injections for persistent radicular pain

Local corticosteroid injections for carpal tunnel or ulnar nerve entrapment

Surgical Treatment

Considered when conservative care fails after 6–12 weeks or if there's progressive weakness or loss of reflexes

Rapidly worsening weakness in the arm or hand

Preventing Nerve-Related Left Arm Pain

Maintain good posture—keep your head aligned over your shoulders

Ergonomic workspace: keyboard and mouse at elbow height, monitor at eye level

Take frequent breaks during repetitive tasks

Perform neck and wrist stretches daily

Manage chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes) to reduce neuropathy risk

Final Thoughts

Left arm pain caused by a nerve issue often presents with distinctive burning, tingling, or shooting pain that follows a nerve pathway and may include weakness or numbness. Early recognition, proper diagnosis and timely treatment can prevent long-term nerve damage and restore function.

If you experience any life-threatening symptoms—or if your pain is severe, worsening, or accompanied by significant weakness—please speak to a doctor right away.
