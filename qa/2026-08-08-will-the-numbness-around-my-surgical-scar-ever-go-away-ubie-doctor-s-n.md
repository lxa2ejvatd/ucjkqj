# Will the Numbness Around My Surgical Scar Ever Go Away? | Ubie Doctor's Note

Will the Numbness Around My Surgical Scar Ever Go Away?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Numbness around a surgical scar is a normal part of healing. Most people experience gradual improvement over weeks to months, with continued nerve recovery often extending up to a year. Several factors influence how quickly sensation returns, including:

Age and overall health

Underlying conditions such as diabetes or vascular disease

Nutritional status, particularly B vitamins and protein intake

Self-care techniques like gentle scar massage and sensory re-education exercises

While mild numbness is expected, persistent or worsening symptoms—such as spreading numbness, sharp pain, or signs of infection—warrant a follow-up with your healthcare provider.

Explanation

Experiencing numbness around a surgical scar is common. When your body heals, nerves that were cut or stretched during surgery need time to repair themselves. While this process can be slow, most people see improvement over weeks to months—and sometimes up to a year. Below, we'll explain why numbness happens, what to expect, and practical steps you can take.

Why Numbness Around a Surgical Scar Occurs

During surgery, small nerves in the skin can be:

Stretched or compressed by retractors and instruments

Cut directly by scalpels or electrocautery

Damaged by swelling, bleeding, or scar tissue

When these nerves are injured, they can't send normal signals to your brain. This results in the area feeling dull, tingly, or completely numb.

Typical Timeline for Nerve Recovery

Nerve healing is a gradual process. The rate of regeneration is often compared to "one inch per month," but individual results vary:

First few weeks: Swelling goes down, and you may notice tingling or "pins and needles."

1–3 months: Nerves begin to reconnect. Sensation often returns in patches.

3–12 months: Most people see steady improvement. Small areas of numbness may persist.

After 12 months: Further gains are possible but tend to be slower.

Keep in mind that deeper or more extensive nerve damage can lengthen recovery.

Factors That Affect Healing

Several factors influence how quickly—and how fully—sensation returns:

Age: Younger people usually heal faster.

Health conditions: Diabetes, poor circulation, or autoimmune disorders can slow nerve repair.

Smoking: Nicotine reduces blood flow and delays healing.

Nutrition: Adequate protein, vitamins B12 and D, and minerals like zinc support nerve health.

Physical activity: Gentle movement encourages blood flow without overstressing the healing area.

What's Normal vs. When to Worry

Some change in sensation is expected. Here's what's generally normal:

Persistent but improving numbness

Occasional tingling or slight burning sensations

Mild itching as nerves reawaken

However, you should contact your healthcare provider if you notice:

Increasing numbness or spreading loss of feeling

Severe, unrelenting pain or sharp shooting pain

Signs of infection around the scar (redness, warmth, discharge, fever)

Nerves don't heal overnight. It's normal to feel frustrated if progress is slow. Tracking small improvements—like the return of light touch or slight temperature changes—can help you see gains that might otherwise go unnoticed.

When to Follow Up with Your Doctor

Keep your surgeon or primary care provider in the loop if you experience:

No improvement after 6–12 months

Worsening symptoms or new areas of numbness

Any signs of infection or wound breakdown

Concerns about scar tissue becoming hard or painful

Always "speak to a doctor" if you have symptoms that are life threatening, like severe pain, fever, or sudden loss of function. Your care team can decide if advanced testing—such as nerve conduction studies or imaging—is needed.
